# NM-10.5: Boundary Conditions in PDEs

> 🟠 **Priority:** Secondary | **Tested:** 2024 Q.4(c) | **Typical marks:** 3

## What This Is (and Why It Matters)

A partial differential equation describes only how quantities bend, curve, or change relative to their neighbors inside a region. It does not know where the edges of the universe are, nor what holds them in place! 

For example, Laplace's equation $\nabla^2 u = 0$ is satisfied by infinitely many different functions: $u = 5$, $u = 3x - 2y$, $u = x^2 - y^2$, $u = e^x \sin y$, or $u = \ln(x^2 + y^2)$. Which one is the real temperature of your microchip? 

You cannot know until you specify what happens at the borders. **Boundary conditions provide the physical anchors that pin down one single, unique solution.**

In numerical computing, boundary conditions are even more crucial. If you try to run finite differences without boundary conditions, your linear system has more unknowns than equations, the matrix is singular, and your computer code crashes with a division-by-zero error.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Boundary Value Problem (BVP):** "A differential equation together with a set of conditions specified at more than one point (usually on the boundary of the domain of interest) is called a boundary value problem." -- Jain, §9.1 (p. 343)
>
> **In simple words:** A problem where you are given a rule for the inside of a shape, along with fixed measurements around its perimeter, and asked to find the values everywhere in between.

> **Dirichlet Boundary Condition (First Kind):** "Given a continuous function $f$ on the boundary $C$ of a region $R$, it is required to find $u(x,y)$ such that $u = f$ on $C$." -- Jain, §9.1 (p. 343), Eq. (9.6)
>
> **In simple words:** You directly specify the exact value of the unknown function (such as voltage $V = 100\text{ V}$ or temperature $T = 50^\circ\text{C}$) along the boundary edge.

> **Neumann Boundary Condition (Second Kind):** "A condition that specifies the value of the normal derivative of the unknown function along the boundary: $\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = g$ on $C$." -- Jain, §9.1 (p. 343)
>
> **In simple words:** Instead of fixing the temperature itself, you fix the heat flow (flux) or rate of change across the boundary (for example, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = 0$ on a perfectly insulated wall).

> **Robin / Mixed Boundary Condition (Third Kind):** "A condition specifying a linear combination of the function value and its normal derivative: $a u + b \frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = c$ on $C$." -- Jain, §9.1
>
> **In simple words:** A boundary where convective heat transfer or radiation occurs, so the temperature and heat flux are tied together.

## The Core Idea

### Why Boundary Conditions Are Mandatory in Numerical PDEs

When solving PDEs numerically using finite difference grids, boundary conditions serve four indispensable mathematical and practical roles:

![A mixed Dirichlet mesh with top edge non-zero and other edges zero](../diagrams/nm10_laplace_nine_point_boundary_grid.jpg)

#### 1. Ensuring Unique Solvability (Preventing Singular Matrices)
A second-order PDE like $\nabla^2 u = 0$ has two degrees of freedom in each spatial dimension. 
If you set up a finite difference stencil $u_{i+1,j} + u_{i-1,j} + u_{i,j+1} + u_{i,j-1} - 4u_{i,j} = 0$ without boundary equations, every row sums to $1 + 1 + 1 + 1 - 4 = 0$.
The sum of every row in the matrix is zero, which means the matrix determinant is zero ($\det A = 0$). The system is singular and admits an infinite family of arbitrary solutions. Imposing boundary conditions replaces outer unknowns with fixed constants, restoring full rank ($\det A \ne 0$) and guaranteeing exactly one unique solution vector.

#### 2. Suppressing Error and Preventing Unstable Growth
Numerical algorithms propagate information across grid nodes. If boundary conditions are improperly formulated or ill-posed, small numerical round-off errors generated at one node amplify exponentially as they sweep across the grid. A well-posed boundary condition acts as an energy sink that absorbs errors and guarantees stable convergence.

#### 3. Supplying Missing Equations on the Perimeter
When you place a five-point stencil on an interior node right next to the boundary (say, row $j = 1$), the stencil looks for its South neighbor at $j = 0$. But $j = 0$ lies outside the interior problem! The boundary condition supplies the exact numerical value needed for that South neighbor, closing the equation.

#### 4. Corner Point Consistency
At the four corner vertices of a rectangular plate, two boundary edges meet. 
For instance, if the top edge is held at $100\text{ V}$ and the right edge is held at $50\text{ V}$, what is the voltage at the corner $(3, 3)$?
In physical experiments, an insulating gap is placed at the corners. In numerical methods, if two conflicting boundary values are forced onto the exact same corner node, the solver encounters a discontinuity that can corrupt adjacent interior nodes. A well-defined problem specifies compatible corner data.

## 🏆 Past Exam Questions

### 2024 Q.4(c): State the Importance of Boundary Conditions in Solving PDEs Numerically -- [03 marks]

**Question:** State the importance of boundary conditions in solving PDEs numerically.

**Let's write out the full answer.**

#### Part 1: Why Boundary Conditions Decide the Answer
A partial differential equation gives the behaviour **inside** a region. Boundary conditions say what happens at the edges. Together with the PDE they form a complete problem; without them the problem is under-determined and the numerical solution is not unique.

#### Part 2: What Breaks Without Them
1. **Non-uniqueness:** Second-order equations need two conditions. Supply one and the solution is still not fixed.
2. **Unstable growth:** An error at a boundary, or any small error at all, grows as it is carried across the grid, so a badly posed problem produces a wrong answer that grows with the grid size.
3. **Solvability:** The linear system from the difference equations is singular without boundary equations, so standard solvers fail or return nonsense.

#### Part 3: Their Role in Practice
1. They supply the missing end rows when the interior difference equations leave the two outermost rows undefined.
2. They are enforced **exactly**, not approximately. Imposing $y(0) = 0$ to four significant figures injects that same error into every computed value.
3. Corner points, where two boundary curves meet, must satisfy **both** conditions. If they disagree at the corner the problem has no solution, and this is a common cause of a solver reporting a singular matrix.
4. The type of condition matters. For a diffusion problem, prescribing $u$ (Dirichlet) is stable, while prescribing its derivative (Neumann) requires a compatibility check on the corner data.

$$
\boxed{\text{PDE} + \text{boundary conditions} = \text{a problem with one unique numerical solution}}
$$

## ⚡ Quick Review

**The concept in one sentence:** A PDE provides the internal physical laws of a domain, but boundary conditions supply the constraints needed to produce a unique, stable numerical solution.

**The three classic types of boundary conditions:**
- **Dirichlet:** Prescribes the value of $u$ on the boundary ($u = f$).
- **Neumann:** Prescribes the normal flux / derivative on the boundary ($\frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = g$).
- **Robin / Mixed:** Prescribes a combination of $u$ and its derivative ($a u + b \frac{\partial u}{\partial n} = c$).

**The four hazards of missing boundary conditions:**
1. Singular coefficient matrix ($\det A = 0$).
2. Infinite non-unique solutions.
3. Exponential error growth across the mesh.
4. Missing values for perimeter stencil arms.

**Time estimate in exam:** 5 to 6 minutes for a complete theory answer.
