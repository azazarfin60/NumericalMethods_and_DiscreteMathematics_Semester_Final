# NM-2.6: Method Comparison and Convergence

> 🔴 **Priority:** Critical | **Tested:** 3/8 years + CT-01 | **Typical marks:** 4–6

## What This Is (and Why It Matters)

Now that you have seen all four classic root-finding methods (Bisection, False Position, Secant, and Newton-Raphson), how do you choose which one to use?

Numerical analysis is not a random collection of formulas. It is a carefully balanced trade-off ladder: on one end of the ladder, you have total safety and guaranteed convergence, but slow speed; on the other end, you have lightning-fast quadratic convergence, but zero safety guarantees. 

In RUET exams, this comparison is a prized question. In 2020 Q.2(a), an examiner awarded 6 full marks just for defining roots and writing out the complete four-method comparison matrix. In class tests, examiners ask which method belongs inside a safety-critical autopilot or nuclear reactor trip controller. If you memorize the comparison table in this note, you can capture full marks in five minutes.

---

## 📝 Key Definitions

> **Root of an equation:** A value $x^*$ such that $f(x^*) = 0$. On a Cartesian plot, the curve $y = f(x)$ crosses or touches the horizontal axis at $x^*$.
> 
> **In simple words:** The exact $x$-coordinate where the function's graph strikes zero.

> **Simple root vs. Multiple root:** A root is **simple** if $f'(x^*) \ne 0$ (the curve crosses cleanly through the axis). A root has **multiplicity $m > 1$** if $f(x^*) = 0$ and $f'(x^*) = 0$ (the curve flattens or turns around at the axis).
> 
> **In simple words:** A simple root is a clean crossing point. A multiple root is a curve touching the axis and bouncing back (like $y = x^2$ at $x = 0$), which makes numerical solvers crawl.

> **Explicit root vs. Implicit root:** An **explicit** root can be written down directly as a closed algebraic formula (like $x = \sqrt{2}$). An **implicit** root cannot be solved analytically (like $x^5 - x - 1 = 0$) and can only be evaluated numerically.
> 
> **In simple words:** Explicit means you can write the exact formula on paper. Implicit means you are forced to use an iterative computer algorithm.

> **Order of convergence ($p$):** If the sequence of errors satisfies $\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx C \varepsilon_n^p$ as $n \to \infty$, the exponent $p$ is called the order of convergence.
> 
> **In simple words:** The speed rating of an algorithm. If $p = 1$, it is linear (constant digit gain); if $p = 1.618$, it is superlinear; if $p = 2$, it is quadratic (doubling correct digits every single step).

---

## The Core Idea

### The Safe-to-Fast Ladder

Every method in this chapter occupies a specific rung on the engineering trade-off ladder. Each higher rung trades safety for speed:

1. **Rung 1 -- Bisection:** Uses only the sign change of the interval. Completely ignores function values and slopes. Cannot fail under any circumstances. Linear convergence ($p = 1$).
2. **Rung 2 -- False Position (Regula Falsi):** Keeps the sign-changing bracket, but draws a chord between the endpoints to guess where zero lies. Faster than bisection, but can suffer from one-sided stagnation on curved functions. Linear convergence ($p = 1$).
3. **Rung 3 -- Secant Method:** Throws the bracket away completely. Draws a chord through the last two iterates and always discards the oldest point. Never stagnates. Superlinear convergence ($p = 1.618$).
4. **Rung 4 -- Newton-Raphson:** Uses only a single point, but demands its exact tangent slope $f'(x)$. Blazingly fast quadratic convergence ($p = 2$). However, it can blow up near flat regions or diverge if started poorly.

$$
\boxed{\text{Bracketing methods are safe and slow; open methods are fast and unguaranteed}}
$$

## Understanding Convergence Rates in Depth

Examiners love to test whether you actually understand what the numbers in the order column mean:

### Bisection: Order 1 with Known Constant
The error satisfies $\varepsilon_{n+1} = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_n$. The constant $C = 0.5$ is completely independent of the function. You gain about 1 decimal digit every $3.3$ iterations ($\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$). The total workload is completely predictable in advance.

### False Position: Order 1 with Unknown Constant
The error is linear ($\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx C \varepsilon_n$), but the constant $C$ depends on function curvature. On nearly linear functions, $C \ll 0.5$ (very fast). But on convex functions, one endpoint freezes, $C \to 1.0$, and the method crawls slower than bisection!

### Secant: Order 1.618 (The Golden Ratio)
The convergence order is $p = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$. Because it requires only **one new function evaluation per step** (reusing the previous $f_{i-1}$), its effective efficiency index is:
$$
\text{Efficiency} = p^{1/\text{cost}} = (1.618)^1 \approx 1.618
$$
Compare this to Newton-Raphson, which has order $p = 2$ but costs **two evaluations per step** ($f$ and $f'$):
$$
\text{Efficiency} = p^{1/\text{cost}} = 2^{1/2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414
$$
**Surprise:** On computers where calculating derivatives is computationally expensive, the Secant method is actually more efficient per function evaluation than Newton-Raphson!

### Newton-Raphson: Order 2 (Quadratic)
The error is squared on every step ($\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx C \varepsilon_n^2$). Near a simple root, 3 to 4 iterations achieve double-precision machine accuracy ($10^{-16}$).

## The Industrial Practice: Hybrid Algorithms

In real-world engineering software (like MATLAB's `fzero` or SciPy's `brentq`), no one uses pure Newton-Raphson or pure Bisection alone:
- **Pure Newton** is too dangerous (it can blow up or divide by zero).
- **Pure Bisection** is too slow.

**The Golden Engineering Rule (Brent's Hybrid):**
1. Trap the root in a guaranteed sign-changing bracket $[a, b]$.
2. Attempt a fast open step (Newton-Raphson or inverse quadratic interpolation).
3. If the step lands comfortably inside the bracket and shrinks the error rapidly, accept it!
4. If the step lands outside the bracket or fails to cut the interval sufficiently, discard it immediately and execute a safe Bisection step.

This hybrid approach gives you the best of both worlds: the blazing quadratic speed of open methods combined with the 100% convergence guarantee of bracketing methods.

## 🔧 Worked Example: Head-to-Head Showdown on $x^{2.2} = 69$

> **Question:** Compare how Bisection, False Position, and Secant perform when solving $x^{2.2} - 69 = 0$ on the interval $[5, 8]$ to four decimal places.

**Let's analyze the performance:**

1. **Closed-form exact root:**
$$
x = 69^{1/2.2} = 69^{5/11} \approx 6.85236512
$$
2. **False Position (Regula Falsi):**
   Takes **7 iterations** to reach $6.85237$. Because $f(x)$ is strongly convex, the upper endpoint $b = 8.000000$ remains pinned across all seven steps.
3. **Secant Method:**
   Takes **5 iterations** starting from $x_0 = 5, x_1 = 8$ to reach $6.852365$. Because it is not constrained to maintain a bracket, it avoids endpoint freezing and glides straight to the root with order $1.618$.
4. **Bisection Method:**
   Starting with bracket width $8 - 5 = 3$, achieving tolerance $10^{-4}$ requires:
$$
n \ge \log_2\left(\frac{3}{10^{-4}}\right) = \log_2(30000) \approx 14.87 \implies 15 \text{ iterations}
$$
   In practice, it takes **18 iterations** to match five decimal places.

**Conclusion:** Secant is the fastest (5 steps). Regula Falsi suffers from one-sided stagnation (7 steps). Bisection is the slowest (18 steps), but its error bound was guaranteed in advance before taking a single step!

### CT-01 Q.2(a): Compare Bisection and Regula Falsi for a safety-critical system [05 marks]

**The question:** A chemical plant uses a computerized system to find the operating temperature where the reaction pressure becomes zero, modeled by a continuous nonlinear equation $f(T) = 0$. The engineers must choose between the Bisection Method and the Regula Falsi Method. Compare the Bisection Method and Regula Falsi Method in terms of the required condition for application, convergence behavior, and suitability for a safety-critical system.

**How to answer like a top student:**
Frame your answer around deterministic time bounds and predictability versus speed.

**Solution:**

In safety-critical applications (such as flight control surfaces or nuclear reactor shutdown rods), **predictability is more important than raw speed**:
1. **Deterministic execution:** Bisection guarantees an exact mathematical bound on the required iterations:
$$
n = \left\lceil \log_2\left(\frac{b - a}{\varepsilon}\right) \right\rceil
$$
This allows real-time safety software to prove worst-case execution time (WCET). Regula Falsi offers no deterministic iteration bound.
2. **Immunity to function pathology:** Strongly curved functions cause Regula Falsi to freeze on one endpoint, creating unexpected delays. Bisection is completely immune to function curvature.
3. **Verdict:** Bisection is the strictly preferred method for hard real-time safety interlocks.

---

### CT-01 Q.2(b): Why does one endpoint stall in Regula Falsi, and how is it repaired? [05 marks]

**The question:** During testing, the Regula Falsi Method repeatedly updates only one endpoint of the interval, leading to slow convergence. Explain why this happens, compare it with the Bisection Method, and suggest a suitable improvement.

**How to answer like a top student:**
Explain the geometric convexity mechanism and define the Illinois modification with its halving rule.

**Solution:**

**Why stagnation happens:**
When $f(x)$ is strongly convex or concave across $[a, b]$, the straight chord always stays on one side of the curve. So, the chord's $x$-intercept always lands on the same side of the true root. The sign update rule replaces the same boundary every single iteration, leaving the opposite boundary pinned indefinitely ($b$ never moves).

**The Illinois Remedy:**
When an endpoint fails to update for two consecutive iterations, divide its stored function value by 2:
$$
f_{\text{stuck}} \leftarrow \frac{1}{2} f_{\text{stuck}}
$$
This pulls the chord down, forcing the next intercept to land across the root, un-freezing both boundaries and restoring rapid convergence while preserving 100% bracket safety.

## ⚡ Quick Review

**The method in one sentence:** Root finding balances safety against speed on a four-rung ladder: Bisection and Regula Falsi guarantee convergence inside a bracket, while Secant and Newton-Raphson chase roots with superlinear and quadratic speed.

**The formula you must memorize:**
$$
\text{Bisection: } p = 1, \quad \text{Secant: } p \approx 1.618, \quad \text{Newton: } p = 2
$$

**The most common exam mistake:** Stating that Newton-Raphson is always superior. Newton requires two function evaluations per step and fails at $f'(x) = 0$, whereas Secant costs only one evaluation and needs no derivative.

**Time estimate in exam:** 6–8 minutes for the full 6-mark comparison table.
