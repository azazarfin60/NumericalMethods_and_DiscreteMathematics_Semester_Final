## 4.4 METHOD OF LEAST SQUARES FOR CONTINUOUS FUNCTIONS

In the previous sections, we considered the least squares approximations of discrete data. We shall, in the present section, discuss the least squares approximation of a continuous function on $[a, b]$. The summations in the normal equations are now replaced by definite integrals.

<!-- Page 141 -->

Let
$$y(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n \tag{4.37}$$
be chosen to minimize
$$S(a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n) = \int_a^b W(x) [y(x) - (a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n)]^2\, dx. \tag{4.38}$$
The necessary conditions for a minimum are given by
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial a_0} = \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_1} = \dots = \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_n} = 0, \tag{4.39}$$
which yield
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
-2 \int_a^b W(x) [y(x) - (a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n)]\, dx &= 0 \\
-2 \int_a^b W(x) [y(x) - (a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n)] x\, dx &= 0 \\
-2 \int_a^b W(x) [y(x) - (a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n)] x^2\, dx &= 0 \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
-2 \int_a^b W(x) [y(x) - (a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_n x^n)] x^n\, dx &= 0.
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{4.40}
$$
Rearrangement of terms in Eq. (4.40) gives the system
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
a_0 \int_a^b W(x)\, dx + a_1 \int_a^b x W(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b x^n W(x)\, dx &= \int_a^b W(x) y(x)\, dx \\
a_0 \int_a^b x W(x)\, dx + a_1 \int_a^b x^2 W(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b x^{n+1} W(x)\, dx &= \int_a^b x W(x) y(x)\, dx \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
a_0 \int_a^b x^n W(x)\, dx + a_1 \int_a^b x^{n+1} W(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b x^{2n} W(x)\, dx &= \int_a^b x^n W(x) y(x)\, dx.
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{4.41}
$$
The system in Eq. (4.41) comprises $(n + 1)$ normal equations in $(n + 1)$ unknowns, viz. $a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ and they always possess a 'unique' solution.

> [!NOTE] EXAMPLE 4.11
> Construct a least squares quadratic approximation to the function $y(x) = \sin x$ on $[0,\, \pi/2]$ with respect to the weight function $W(x) = 1$.

> be the required quadratic approximation. Then using Eq. (4.41), we obtain the system

### 4.4.1 Orthogonal Polynomials

In the previous section, we have seen that the method of determining a least square approximation to a continuous function gives satisfactory results. However, this method possesses the disadvantage of solving a large linear system of equations. Besides, such a system may exhibit a peculiar tendency called *ill-conditioning*, which means that small change in any of its parameters introduces large errors in the solution—the degree of *ill-conditioning* increasing with the order of the system. Hence, alternative methods of solving the aforesaid least-squares problem have gained importance, and of these the method that employs 'orthogonal polynomials' is currently in use. This method possessess the great advantage that it does not require a linear system to be solved and is described below.

We choose the approximation in the form:
$$Y(x) = a_0 f_0(x) + a_1 f_1(x) + \dots + a_n f_n(x), \tag{4.42}$$
where $f_j(x)$ is a polynomial in $x$ of degree $j$.

Then, we write
$$S(a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n) = \int_a^b W(x) \left\{ y(x) - [a_0 f_0(x) + a_1 f_1(x) + \dots + a_n f_n(x)] \right\}^2\, dx. \tag{4.43}$$
For $S$ to be minimum, we must have
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial a_0} &= 0 = -2 \int_a^b W(x) \left\{ y(x) - [a_0 f_0(x) + a_1 f_1(x) + \dots + a_n f_n(x)] \right\} f_0(x)\, dx \\
\frac{\partial S}{\partial a_1} &= 0 = -2 \int_a^b W(x) \left\{ y(x) - [a_0 f_0(x) + a_1 f_1(x) + \dots + a_n f_n(x)] \right\} f_1(x)\, dx \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
\frac{\partial S}{\partial a_n} &= 0 = -2 \int_a^b W(x) \left\{ y(x) - [a_0 f_0(x) + a_1 f_1(x) + \dots + a_n f_n(x)] \right\} f_n(x)\, dx
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{4.44}
$$

<!-- Page 144 -->

The normal equations are now given by
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
a_0 \int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx &+ a_1 \int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_1(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_n(x)\, dx \\
&= \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_0(x)\, dx \\
a_0 \int_a^b W(x) f_1(x) f_0(x)\, dx &+ a_1 \int_a^b W(x) f_1^2(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b W(x) f_1(x) f_n(x)\, dx \\
&= \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_1(x)\, dx \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
a_0 \int_a^b W(x) f_n(x) f_0(x)\, dx &+ a_1 \int_a^b W(x) f_n(x) f_1(x)\, dx + \dots + a_n \int_a^b W(x) f_n^2(x)\, dx \\
&= \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_n(x)\, dx.
\end{aligned}
\right\} \tag{4.45}
$$
The above system can be written more simply as
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_0 \int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_j(x)\, dx &+ a_1 \int_a^b W(x) f_1(x) f_j(x)\, dx + \dots \\
&+ a_n \int_a^b W(x) f_n(x) f_j(x)\, dx = \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_j(x)\, dx, \quad j = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.46}
$$
In Eq. (4.45), we find products of the type $f_p(x) f_q(x)$ in the integrands, and if we assume that
$$
\int_a^b W(x) f_p(x) f_q(x)\, dx = \begin{cases}
0, & p \neq q \\
\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_p^2(x)\, dx, & p = q,
\end{cases} \tag{4.47}
$$
then the system (4.45) reduces to
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_0 \int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx &= \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_0(x)\, dx \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
a_n \int_a^b W(x) f_n^2(x)\, dx &= \int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_n(x)\, dx.
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- Page 145 -->

From the preceding equations we obtain
$$a_j = \frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) y(x) f_j(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_j^2(x)\, dx}, \quad j = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n. \tag{4.48}$$
Substitution of $a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n$ in Eq. (4.42) then yields the required least squares approximation, but the functions $f_0(x), f_1(x), \dots, f_n(x)$ are still not known. The $f_j(x)$, which are polynomials in $x$ satisfying the conditions (4.47), are called *orthogonal polynomials* and are said to be orthogonal with respect to the weight function $W(x)$. They play an important role in numerical analysis and a few of them are listed below in Table 4.1.

**Table 4.1 Orthogonal Polynomials\***

| Name | $f_j(x)$ | Interval | $W(x)$ |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Jacobi | $P_n^{(\alpha, \beta)}(x)$ | $[-1,\, 1]$ | $(1 - x)^\alpha (1 + x)^\beta \ (\alpha, \beta > -1)$ |
| Chebyshev (first kind) | $T_n(x)$ | $[-1,\, 1]$ | $(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}$ |
| Chebyshev (second kind) | $U_n(x)$ | $[-1,\, 1]$ | $(1 - x^2)^{1/2}$ |
| Legendre | $P_n(x)$ | $(-1,\, 1)$ | $1$ |
| Laguerre | $L_n(x)$ | $[0,\, \infty)$ | $e^{-x}$ |
| Hermite | $H_n(x)$ | $(-\infty,\, \infty)$ | $e^{-x^2}$ |

A brief discussion of some important properties of the Chebyshev polynomials $T_n(x)$ and their usefulness in the approximation of functions will be given in a later section of this chapter. We now return to our discussion of the problem of determining the least squares approximation. As we noted earlier, the functions $f_j(x)$ are yet to be determined. These are obtained by using the 'Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process,' which has important applications in numerical analysis. This process is described in the next section.

### 4.4.2 Gram–Schmidt Orthogonalization Process

Suppose that the orthogonal polynomial $f_i(x)$, valid on the interval $[a, b]$, has the leading term $x^i$. Then, starting with
$$f_0(x) = 1 \tag{4.49}$$

<!-- Page 146 -->

we find that the linear polynomial $f_1(x)$, with leading term $x$, can be written as
$$f_1(x) = x + k_{1,0} f_0(x), \tag{4.50}$$
where $k_{1,0}$ is a constant to be determined. Since $f_1(x)$ and $f_0(x)$ are orthogonal, we have
$$\int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_1(x)\, dx = 0 = \int_a^b x W(x) f_0(x)\, dx + k_{1,0} \int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx$$
using Eqs. (4.47) and (4.49). From the above, we obtain
$$k_{1,0} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x W(x) f_0(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx} \tag{4.51}$$
and Eq. (4.50) gives
$$f_1(x) = x - \frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x W(x) f_0(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx}.$$
Now, the polynomial $f_2(x)$, of degree 2 in $x$ and with leading term $x^2$, may be written as
$$f_2(x) = x^2 + k_{2,0} f_0(x) + k_{2,1} f_1(x), \tag{4.52}$$
where the constants $k_{2,0}$ and $k_{2,1}$ are to be determined by using the orthogonality conditions in Eq. (4.47). Since $f_2(x)$ is orthogonal to $f_0(x)$, we have
$$\int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) [x^2 + k_{2,0} f_0(x) + k_{2,1} f_1(x)]\, dx = 0.$$
Since $\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_1(x)\, dx = 0$, the above equation gives
$$k_{2,0} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x^2 W(x) f_0(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_0^2(x)\, dx} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x^2 W(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x)\, dx}. \tag{4.53}$$

<!-- Page 147 -->

Again, since $f_2(x)$ is orthogonal to $f_1(x)$, we have
$$\int_a^b W(x) f_1(x) [x^2 + k_{2,0} f_0(x) + k_{2,1} f_1(x)]\, dx = 0.$$
Using the condition that $\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_0(x) f_1(x)\, dx = 0$, the above yields
$$k_{2,1} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x^2 W(x) f_1(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_1^2(x)\, dx}. \tag{4.54}$$
Since $k_{2,0}$ and $k_{2,1}$ are known, Eq. (4.52) determines $f_2(x)$. Proceeding in this way, the method can be generalized and we write
$$f_j(x) = x^j + k_{j,0} f_0(x) + k_{j,1} f_1(x) + \dots + k_{j, j-1} f_{j-1}(x), \tag{4.55}$$
where the constants $k_{j,i}$ are so chosen that $f_j(x)$ is orthogonal to $f_0(x), f_1(x), \dots, f_{j-1}(x)$. These conditions yield
$$k_{j,i} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x^j W(x) f_i(x)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b W(x) f_i^2(x)\, dx}. \tag{4.56}$$
Since the $a_i$ and $f_i(x)$ in Eq. (4.42) are known, the approximation $Y(x)$ can now be determined. The following example illustrates the method of procedure.

> [!NOTE] EXAMPLE 4.12
> Obtain the first-four orthogonal polynomials $f_n(x)$ on $[-1,\, 1]$ with respect to the weight function $W(x) = 1$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> Let $f_0(x) = 1$. Then Eq. (4.51) gives
> $$k_{1,0} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 dx} = 0.$$
> We then obtain from Eq. (4.50), $f_1(x) = x$. Equations (4.53) and (4.54) give respectively
> $$k_{2,0} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^2\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 dx} = -\frac{1}{3}$$

<!-- Page 148 -->

> and
> $$k_{2,1} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^2 x\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^2\, dx} = 0.$$
> Then Eq. (4.52) yields $f_2(x) = x^2 - 1/3$.
> In a similar manner, we obtain
> $$k_{3,0} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^3\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 dx} = 0,$$
> $$k_{3,1} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^3 x\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^2\, dx} = -\frac{3}{5},$$
> $$k_{3,2} = -\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 x^3 (x^2 - 1/3)\, dx}{\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 (x^2 - 1/3)^2\, dx} = 0.$$
> It is easily verified that
> $$f_3(x) = x^3 - \frac{3}{5}x.$$
> Thus the required orthogonal polynomials are $1$, $x$, $x^2 - 1/3$ and $x^3 - (3/5)x$. These polynomials are called *Legendre polynomials* and are usually denoted by $P_n(x)$. It is easy to verify that these polynomials satisfy the orthogonal property given in Eq. (4.47). An important application of Legendre polynomials occurs in numerical quadrature (see Chapter 6).
