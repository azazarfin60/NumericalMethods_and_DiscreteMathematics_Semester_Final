## Class Test 01

**Modeling, truncation and round-off errors · bisection and regula falsi**

### **[CT-01 Q.1(a)] Develop a mathematical model representing the temperature estimation problem and identify the assumptions made.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

**The model.** Let $T(t)$ be the reactor's temperature at time $t$, in kelvin or
degrees Celsius. For a well-mixed reactor the energy balance per unit mass is

$$C_p \frac{dT}{dt} = Q(t) - hA\bigl(T - T_a(t)\bigr)$$

where $C_p$ is the specific heat capacity, $Q(t)$ the heat input from the
reaction, $h$ the convective heat-transfer coefficient, $A$ the internal surface
area, and $T_a(t)$ the ambient or coolant temperature. This is a first-order
initial value problem in $T$, and it is the model the software approximates.

**The exponential estimate.** The software evaluates the exponential with the
first four non-zero Taylor terms. In dimensionless form, for
$z = (T - T_a)/T_a$,

$$e^z \approx 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2!} + \frac{z^3}{3!}$$

and the remainder is $\dfrac{z^4 e^{\xi}}{4!}$ for some $\xi \in (0, z)$, so the
truncation error grows like $z^4$.

**The assumptions.** Each is a modelling decision that, if wrong, shows up as a
mismatch between prediction and measurement:

1. **The reactor is well mixed.** Temperature is uniform throughout the volume,
   so a single state variable describes it. A real reactor with a measurable
   internal gradient needs a distributed model, and lumping it is the first
   approximation to fail.
2. **$C_p$, $h$ and $A$ are constant.** Properties are treated as independent of
   temperature over the operating range. For a gas $C_p$ varies appreciably, and
   for a fouled surface $h$ falls with time.
3. **$Q(t)$ is known.** The heat release is either measured or computed from the
   reaction stoichiometry, and is not itself in doubt. This is the assumption
   most likely to be wrong in a reactor whose composition is changing.
4. **Heat loss is linear in the temperature difference.** Newton's law of
   cooling, $hA(T - T_a)$, with a constant coefficient. Radiation contributes a
   $T^4$ term that this omits, and at high temperature radiation is not a
   correction but a term of the same order.
5. **Ambient conditions are measured, not predicted.** $T_a(t)$ is taken as
   known, so the model has no weather uncertainty in it.
6. **No heat is generated by the measurement or by stirring.** Sensors and
   agitation are treated as negligible, which is safe only for a large, slowly
   driven vessel.

> [!info] Check by the steady state
> Setting $dT/dt = 0$ gives $T_{ss} = T_a + Q/hA$, which is the correct
> energy-balance equilibrium for a first-order system: one exponential approach
> with time constant $\tau = C_p/hA$ and no overshoot. Any other shape, a
> two-stage response or an overshoot, means at least one assumption has failed,
> and this test localises the failure to the model rather than to the arithmetic.
>
> The time constant is also what the step size must respect. A numerical method
> with step $h$ resolves a time constant $\tau$ correctly only when $h \ll \tau$,
> so $h = \tau/10$ is the usual starting point.

> [!warning] Where this model is weakest, and what follows for the software
> Assumption 4 is the one that bites here. Radiation loss scales as $T^4$ and the
> model omits it entirely, so the *model* under-predicts the true cooling rate
> and the *software* is being asked to be accurate about a quantity that is
> itself wrong. No amount of precision in the exponential fixes that, which is the
> reason part (b) asks about error sources separately from this part's modelling.

### **[CT-01 Q.1(b)] Identify at least three possible sources of numerical error in the software and explain how each affects the final prediction.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

The machine stores only **5 significant decimal digits**. Every error below is
live in that regime, and the point of the question is that the effects are of
different *kinds*, not merely of different sizes.

**1. Truncation error from the Taylor series.** Discarding the fourth and later
terms of the exponential leaves a remainder $\dfrac{z^4 e^{\xi}}{4!}$. This is a
**systematic** error: it has one sign, it is the same on every cycle, and it does
not average away. Its size grows like $z^4$, so for a reactor running far above
ambient it dominates everything else. It makes the software read slightly low when
truncating from above.

**2. Round-off error from 5-digit storage.** Each of the additions,
multiplications and divisions in the series is performed to five figures and then
discarded. This is **random**, in sign and magnitude, so over many simulations it
partly averages; its accumulated size grows like $\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ independent
roundings, or like $n$ in the worst case where every rounding is biased the same
way. It is bounded by roughly $10^{-5}$ relative per operation.

**3. Catastrophic cancellation.** When $z$ is small, $e^z$ is close to $1$, and
computing it as $1 + z + z^2/2 + z^3/6$ subtracts almost as much as it adds. For
$z = 0.1$ the answer is $1.10517$, but the intermediate partial sums run $1$,
$1.1$, $1.105$, $1.105167$, so leading digits are destroyed before the answer
exists. The relative error in the result is amplified by roughly
$1/(1 - e^{-z})$ times the machine epsilon, which at $z = 0.1$ is about
a factor of $10$.

**4. Truncation error from the time-stepping.** Whatever scheme advances $T$ in
time replaces an integral by a finite sum, with an error proportional to $h^p$
for order $p$. With $h$ too large the discrete solution has a *different* steady
state from the continuous one, which is a modelling error disguised as a
numerical one.

**5. Input and measurement error in $Q$, $h$, $A$, $T_a$.** These propagate
through the model, and the sensitivity to $hA$ is the sharpest: an error
$\epsilon$ in $h$ shifts the steady state by roughly $\epsilon Q/(hA)^2$, which
is *larger* than the steady state itself relative to its own magnitude.

**6. Accumulation over repeated simulations.** If each cycle starts from the
previous cycle's rounded output, the errors compound across cycles rather than
resetting. A single computation with 5 digits is fine; ten thousand chained ones
are not.

> [!info] Check by halving the step and by widening the precision
> Both effects are separable in one experiment. Run the software twice: once at
> half the step with 5 digits, once at full step with 10 digits.
>
> * Halving the step changes the time-stepping error by $2^{-p}$ and leaves the
>   round-off error essentially unchanged.
> * Widening to 10 digits leaves every truncation error unchanged and shrinks the
>   round-off error by $10^5$.
>
> If the prediction moves when the step is halved, the dominant error is
> truncation. If it moves when the precision is widened, the dominant error is
> round-off. If neither moves it, the error is in the *model* or in the inputs,
> which is the answer part (a) prepares for. This single comparison is what turns
> "there are several sources" into a diagnosis.

> [!warning] The trap: the two errors move in opposite directions
> Truncation error shrinks when more terms are kept, when the step is reduced, or
> when a higher-order scheme is used. Round-off error *grows* under every one of
> those, because each is another arithmetic operation to be rounded. So the
> software cannot be made accurate simply by working harder, and there is an
> optimum beyond which precision is traded for accuracy. With 5 digits the
> round-off floor sits near $10^{-5}$ relative, so the Taylor series is worth
> extending only until its remainder falls below about that level, and no
> further.

### **[CT-01 Q.2(a)] Compare the Bisection Method and Regula Falsi Method in terms of the required condition for application, convergence behavior, and suitability for a safety-critical system.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

**Both methods find a root of $f(T) = 0$ by maintaining a bracketing interval, and
the difference between them is entirely in how the next point is chosen.**

| | Bisection | Regula Falsi |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Requires $f(a)f(b) < 0$ | yes | yes |
| How the next point is chosen | the midpoint $c = (a+b)/2$ | the chord's intercept $c = \dfrac{a f(b) - b f(a)}{f(b) - f(a)}$ |
| Number of function values per step | 1 | 1 |
| Convergence rate | guaranteed linear, $\lvert b-a\rvert$ halves every step | linear, but *rate depends on the function* |
| Interval width | strictly decreases, and predictably | one end may stall indefinitely |
| Needs $f$ to be monotone | no | no |
| Preserves the bracket | always | always, unless modified |

**Required condition.** Identical: a sign change across the initial bracket.
Bisection additionally requires only continuity, and it does not care whether the
function is monotone, convex or well behaved. Regula Falsi uses the straight line
through the two endpoints, so it implicitly assumes the function is *reasonably*
close to linear over the bracket. For a strongly curved function the chord's
intercept can be far from the true root.

**Convergence.** Bisection's rate is bounded in advance: after $n$ steps the
interval is $(b-a)/2^n$, so the number of steps for a tolerance $\varepsilon$ is
$\lceil \log_2\lvert b-a\rvert/\varepsilon\rceil$, known before starting.
Regula Falsi is also linear in the interval width but the constant is
function-dependent and can be arbitrarily poor. Its classic failure is the
**stagnating endpoint**: if $f$ is strongly convex on the bracket, the chord
always cuts the *same* side, one endpoint is retained forever, and convergence
degrades to the rate of a single Newton-like step from that end.

**Suitability for a safety-critical system.** This is the part with a real
answer, and it is not "regula falsi is faster".

1. **Predictability beats speed.** For a system that must be shown to converge,
   bisection offers a bound computable in advance; regula falsi offers none.
   A safety case can state "the bracket is at most $\varepsilon$ after $n$
   iterations" and be done.
2. **Bisection cannot be fooled by the function's shape.** A failed sensor or a
   mis-entered coefficient changes the chord in regula falsi but cannot break the
   halving in bisection.
3. **Regula falsi is still worth using, with the Illinois modification.** The
   unmodified method stalls; halving the retained function value at whichever end
> failed to update removes the stalling and restores a guaranteed linear rate.
   With that modification it converges faster than bisection and remains
   bracketed.
4. **Never a bracketing method for a critical interlock.** Both are iterative and
   neither certifies a root. A safety-critical trip should act on a sign test or a
   hard bound, with the iteration used to *diagnose* rather than to *decide*.

> [!info] Check by a curved function
> Take $f(T) = T^3 - 2T - 5$ on $[2, 3]$, which is convex on the bracket.
> Bisection halves $[2,3]$ every step and reaches $10^{-3}$ in 10 steps by its
> bound. Unmodified regula falsi on the same bracket retains the upper endpoint
> for several consecutive steps, because the chord lies below $f$ and its
> intercept keeps landing on the same side. The Illinois modification removes
> the stalling in one step. That single function separates the two methods'
> behaviour completely, which is why it is the standard illustration.

### **[CT-01 Q.2(b)] During testing, the Regula Falsi Method repeatedly updates only one endpoint of the interval, leading to slow convergence. Explain why this happens, compare it with the Bisection Method, and suggest a suitable improvement.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

**Why one endpoint stalls.** Write the update as

$$c = b - f(b)\frac{b - a}{f(b) - f(a)}$$

and let $g(x)$ be the **secant slope** $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Then

$$c - b = -\frac{f(b)}{g}$$

Consider a convex $f$ on $[a,b]$ with $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$. Convexity means the
chord lies *above* the curve, so $c$ always falls on the side nearer $a$, and
$f(c) < 0$ always. The update rule is $f(a)f(c) < 0 \Rightarrow a \leftarrow c$,
so **$b$ never moves**. The bracket shrinks only from the $a$ side, and it shrinks
at the rate of a single Newton step from a fixed endpoint rather than by halving.

The mechanism is that $g$ stays roughly constant while $f(b)$ stays fixed at its
original value, so $c$ approaches $b$ geometrically with a ratio set by how much
the curve's slope at $b$ exceeds the chord's. For a strongly curved $f$ that
ratio is close to $1$ and the method crawls. The limit is that the iteration
becomes a Newton step from $b$ alone, which for many functions is fast; the
pathology is the many wasted steps before it gets there.

**Comparison with bisection.** Bisection sets $c = (a+b)/2$ with no reference to
the function values, so both endpoints move, the width halves exactly, and the
iteration count for a tolerance is $\lceil \log_2\lvert b-a\rvert/\varepsilon\rceil$
regardless of the shape of $f$. Regula falsi may converge faster *when it works*
and far slower when it does not, and there is no bound that distinguishes the two
cases in advance. The trade is speed against predictability.

**The suitable improvement: the Illinois modification.** At each step, halve the
retained function value at whichever endpoint did *not* move:

$$f(a) \leftarrow \frac{1}{2}f(a) \quad \text{if } a \text{ was retained}, \qquad f(b) \leftarrow \frac{1}{2}f(b) \quad \text{if } b \text{ was retained}$$

This is exactly the perturbation that removes the stalling. Once $f(b)$ is halved,
the next chord's intercept $c - b = -f(b)/g$ is halved too, so $c$ lands nearer
$b$, and the method restores a guaranteed linear rate better than bisection's.
Three further variants exist and all behave similarly: **Pegasus**, which halves
by $f \leftarrow f(1 - f)$; **Anderson-Björck**, which uses a
$1 - 1/(1 + g)$ factor; and a secant-based restart.

**A second, cheaper improvement.** Before iterating, subdivide the initial
bracket into, say, four equal parts and test each for a sign change. The
sub-interval that brackets the root is usually far more nearly linear, so
regula falsi on it has no stalling to suffer. This costs three extra function
evaluations once, and it is worth doing when the initial bracket is wide and the
function is known to be badly curved.

> [!info] Check that the modification works, by watching the bracket
> The diagnostic is not the number of iterations but **which endpoints move**.
> Run unmodified regula falsi on the convex bracket and watch $b$ sit still for
> several steps: that is the pathology. Run the Illinois version and watch both
> endpoints move from the first step onward. The bracket then shrinks faster than
> bisection's, which is the whole claim being tested.
>
> A second check: the Illinois method must never lose the bracket. At every step
> $f(a)f(c) < 0$ still decides the update, so a root remains bracketed, and the
> only thing that changes is the *weight* used in drawing the chord.

> [!warning] What the modification is not
> Illinois does not make regula falsi a quadratically convergent method like
> Newton-Raphson. It restores a *linear* rate, and a good one, but the guarantee
> is still a guarantee about a bracket rather than about a root estimate. It also
> does not help when the failure is a **mis-signed bracket** to begin with: no
> amount of damping finds a root the initial interval does not contain.

## Class Test 02 (Set A)

**Lagrange and Newton's forward/backward interpolation**

### **[CT-02A Q.1] Use the Lagrange Interpolation Formula to estimate the concentration at 5 km.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

| Distance (km) | 2 | 4 | 7 | 10 |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Concentration (mg/L) | 18 | 26 | 43 | 60 |

**The formula.** Lagrange interpolation at $x$ is

$$P(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} y_i L_i(x), \qquad L_i(x) = \prod_{j \neq i}\frac{x - x_j}{x_i - x_j}$$

Each $L_i$ is $1$ at $x_i$ and $0$ at every other node, which is what guarantees
the interpolant passes through all four points exactly.

**The basis polynomials at $x = 5$.**

$$L_0(5) = \frac{(5-4)(5-7)(5-10)}{(2-4)(2-7)(2-10)} = \frac{(1)(-2)(-5)}{(-2)(-5)(-8)} = \frac{10}{-80} = -\frac{1}{8}$$

$$L_1(5) = \frac{(5-2)(5-7)(5-10)}{(4-2)(4-7)(4-10)} = \frac{(3)(-2)(-5)}{(2)(-3)(-6)} = \frac{30}{36} = \frac{5}{6}$$

$$L_2(5) = \frac{(5-2)(5-4)(5-10)}{(7-2)(7-4)(7-10)} = \frac{(3)(1)(-5)}{(5)(3)(-3)} = \frac{-15}{-45} = \frac{1}{3}$$

$$L_3(5) = \frac{(5-2)(5-4)(5-7)}{(10-2)(10-4)(10-7)} = \frac{(3)(1)(-2)}{(8)(6)(3)} = \frac{-6}{144} = -\frac{1}{24}$$

**Check before using them:** $L_0 + L_1 + L_2 + L_3 = -\frac18 + \frac56 + \frac13 - \frac1{24} = 1$, as it must be
for every $x$. Getting this wrong is the commonest slip, and it is a one-line test.

**The estimate.**

$$P(5) = 18\left(-\frac{1}{8}\right) + 26\left(\frac{5}{6}\right) + 43\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) + 60\left(-\frac{1}{24}\right)$$

$$= -2.25 + 21.6667 + 14.3333 - 2.5$$

$$\boxed{C(5) = 31.25 \ \text{mg/L}}$$

**The independent route.** Newton's divided-difference form of the same four
points gives

| order | divided differences |
| :-- | :-- |
| 0 | $18, 26, 43, 60$ |
| 1 | $4, \frac{17}{3}, \frac{17}{3}$ |
| 2 | $\frac{1}{3}, 0$ |
| 3 | $-\frac{1}{24}$ |

so $P(x) = 18 + 4(x-2) + \frac13(x-2)(x-4) - \frac{1}{24}(x-2)(x-4)(x-7)$, and at
$x = 5$:

$$P(5) = 18 + 4(3) + \frac13(3)(1) - \frac{1}{24}(3)(1)(-2) = 18 + 12 + 1 + 0.25 = 31.25$$

The same value from a different arrangement, so neither arithmetic route is
responsible for it.

> [!info] What the polynomial is, and a warning about it
> Expanding gives $P(x) = -\frac{1}{24}x^3 + \frac78 x^2 - \frac{1}{12}x + 15$.
>
> The **nodes are unevenly spaced**, $2, 4, 7, 10$, with gaps of $2, 3, 3$. This
> is exactly where Lagrange is the right tool and Newton forward differences are
> not: with unequal gaps the difference table has no constant first difference and
> Newton's formula in its forward form does not apply. Lagrange needs no
> arithmetic progression.
>
> $x = 5$ lies **inside** $[2, 10]$, so this is interpolation, not extrapolation,
> and the value carries the data's own reliability rather than an extrapolation
> risk. The cubic is also mild: the true concentration curve is concave, and the
> cubic tracks it without the wild oscillation that high-degree interpolation
> produces on unevenly spaced nodes.

### **[CT-02A Q.2] Use Newton's Forward/Backward Interpolation Formula to estimate the temperature at 1.5 and 7 hours.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

| Time (hours) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| Temperature (°C) | 24 | 27 | 31 | 36 | 42 |

**The difference table.** With $h = 2$ hours:

| order | values |
| :-- | :-- |
| $y$ | 24, 27, 31, 36, 42 |
| $\Delta y$ | 3, 4, 5, 6 |
| $\Delta^2 y$ | 1, 1, 1 |
| $\Delta^3 y$ | 0, 0 |
| $\Delta^4 y$ | 0 |

**Note immediately that the third differences vanish.** The table terminates after
order two, so the data lie exactly on a quadratic, and **Newton's formula is exact
here, not an approximation.** That single observation is the strongest check
available and it costs nothing.

**At 1.5 hours: Newton's forward formula.** $1.5$ is near the *start* of the
table, so measure forward from $x_0 = 0$:

$$p = \frac{x - x_0}{h} = \frac{1.5}{2} = 0.75$$

$$y = y_0 + p\Delta y_0 + \frac{p(p-1)}{2!}\Delta^2 y_0 + \frac{p(p-1)(p-2)}{3!}\Delta^3 y_0$$

$$= 24 + (0.75)(3) + \frac{(0.75)(-0.25)}{2}(1) + 0$$

$$= 24 + 2.25 - 0.09375$$

$$\boxed{T(1.5) = 26.15625 \approx 26.16 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$$

**At 7 hours: Newton's backward formula.** $7$ is near the *end*, so measure
backward from $x_n = 8$:

$$p = \frac{x - x_n}{h} = \frac{7 - 8}{2} = -0.5$$

$$y = y_n + p\nabla y_n + \frac{p(p+1)}{2!}\nabla^2 y_n + \frac{p(p+1)(p+2)}{3!}\nabla^3 y_n$$

$$= 42 + (-0.5)(6) + \frac{(-0.5)(0.5)}{2}(1) + 0$$

$$= 42 - 3 - 0.125$$

$$\boxed{T(7) = 38.875 \approx 38.88 \ ^\circ\text{C}}$$

**The independent check.** The interpolating polynomial through the five points
is $P(x) = \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{5x}{4} + 24$, so $P(1.5) = \frac{837}{32} = 26.15625$
and $P(7) = \frac{311}{8} = 38.875$. Both agree, and they are exact.

> [!warning] The sign of $p$ is where marks are lost here
> Newton's **backward** formula uses $p = (x - x_n)/h$, which is **negative** for
> a point inside the table. Writing $p = (x_n - x)/h = +0.5$ gives $42 + 3 + 0.375
> = 45.375$ instead of $38.875$, and the error is large because the first-order
> term has the opposite sign.
>
> Two habits prevent it. First, the backward formula's argument is a *rising*
> factorial $p(p+1)(p+2)$, whereas the forward formula's is *falling*,
> $p(p-1)(p-2)$. Second, check the sign of $p$ before computing anything: forward
> means $0 \le p < 1$ from the start of the table, backward means
> $-1 < p \le 0$ from the end.
>
> A third trap: at $7$ hours the data *increase* by $6$ per step, so a reading
> below $42$ must exceed $36$ and lie below $42$. The wrong answer $45.375$
> exceeds the last tabulated value and should have been rejected on that basis
> before it was ever written down.

## Class Test 02 (Set B)

**Gauss-Seidel iteration and Runge-Kutta methods**

### **[CT-02B Q.1(a)] Rearrange the equations in a suitable form for the Gauss-Seidel iterative method.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

$$4x + y + z = 7, \qquad x + 5y + 2z = -8, \qquad 2x + y + 6z = 6$$

Solve each equation for its own diagonal unknown:

$$x = \frac{7 - y - z}{4}, \qquad y = \frac{-8 - x - 2z}{5}, \qquad z = \frac{6 - 2x - y}{6}$$

**Check that the system is suitable.** The diagonal entries are $4, 5, 6$ and the
off-diagonal row sums are $2, 3, 3$, so **every row is strictly diagonally
dominant**:

$$\lvert 4\rvert > 1 + 1 = 2, \qquad \lvert 5\rvert > 1 + 2 = 3, \qquad \lvert 6\rvert > 2 + 1 = 3$$

Strict dominance of every row is the sufficient condition for Gauss-Seidel to
converge from any starting vector, so this system is set up for the method and no
reordering can improve it. $\det A = 101 \neq 0$, so the solution is unique.

**The Gauss-Seidel form, written to show the ordering.** Each equation uses the
values *just computed* in the same sweep:

$$x^{(k+1)} = \frac{7 - y^{(k)} - z^{(k)}}{4}$$

$$y^{(k+1)} = \frac{-8 - x^{(k+1)} - 2z^{(k)}}{5}$$

$$z^{(k+1)} = \frac{6 - 2x^{(k+1)} - y^{(k+1)}}{6}$$

The superscripts are the whole content of the method: $x$ uses the old $y$ and
$z$, $y$ uses the **new** $x$ and the old $z$, $z$ uses the **new** $x$ and the
**new** $y$. Gauss-Seidel rather than Jacobi is exactly the two $(k+1)$ superscripts
on the right of the second and third equations.

> [!warning] The one transcription slip that matters
> The source prints $X$ in the second equation where the other two use $x$.
> $X + 5y + 2z = -8$ is the same equation only if $X$ means $x$, and it must,
> since it is one system in three unknowns. Read as a fourth distinct variable
> the system is not square and Gauss-Seidel has nothing to iterate. Treated as $x$,
> as plainly intended, the equation is $x + 5y + 2z = -8$.

### **[CT-02B Q.1(b)] Starting with $x_0 = 0$, $y_0 = 0$, $z_0 = 0$, perform four complete iterations using the Gauss-Seidel method.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

**Iteration 1**, from $(0, 0, 0)$:

$$x_1 = \frac{7 - 0 - 0}{4} = 1.75$$

$$y_1 = \frac{-8 - 1.75 - 2(0)}{5} = \frac{-9.75}{5} = -1.95$$

$$z_1 = \frac{6 - 2(1.75) - (-1.95)}{6} = \frac{6 - 3.5 + 1.95}{6} = \frac{4.45}{6} = 0.741667$$

**Iteration 2:**

$$x_2 = \frac{7 - (-1.95) - 0.741667}{4} = \frac{7 + 1.95 - 0.741667}{4} = \frac{8.208333}{4} = 2.052083$$

$$y_2 = \frac{-8 - 2.052083 - 2(0.741667)}{5} = \frac{-8 - 2.052083 - 1.483333}{5} = \frac{-11.535417}{5} = -2.307083$$

$$z_2 = \frac{6 - 2(2.052083) - (-2.307083)}{6} = \frac{6 - 4.104167 + 2.307083}{6} = \frac{4.202917}{6} = 0.700486$$

**Iteration 3:**

$$x_3 = \frac{7 - (-2.307083) - 0.700486}{4} = \frac{7 + 2.307083 - 0.700486}{4} = \frac{8.606597}{4} = 2.151649$$

$$y_3 = \frac{-8 - 2.151649 - 2(0.700486)}{5} = \frac{-8 - 2.151649 - 1.400972}{5} = \frac{-11.552621}{5} = -2.310524$$

### **[CT-02B Q.1(c)] Calculate the absolute error in $x$, $y$, and $z$ between the third and fourth iterations.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

The absolute errors are the differences between consecutive iterates:

$$\lvert x_4 - x_3\rvert = \lvert 2.160663 - 2.151649\rvert = 0.009014$$

$$\lvert y_4 - y_3\rvert = \lvert -2.299281 - (-2.310524)\rvert = 0.011243$$

$$\lvert z_4 - z_3\rvert = \lvert 0.662992 - 0.667871\rvert = 0.004879$$

$$\boxed{\lvert\Delta x\rvert = 0.009014, \quad \lvert\Delta y\rvert = 0.011243, \quad \lvert\Delta z\rvert = 0.004879}$$

> [!warning] This is not the error in the answer
> The difference between two iterates is the **approximate** error, and it
> measures only the progress of the last sweep. The true error is the difference
> from the exact solution:
> $$\lvert x_4 - 218/101\rvert = 0.002247, \qquad \lvert y_4 + 232/101\rvert = 0.002251, \qquad \lvert z_4 - 67/101\rvert = 0.000374$$
>
> These are a quarter of the consecutive differences, because the errors are still
> contracting at about half per sweep. Both numbers are legitimate and the
> question asks for the first, but they must not be confused: the approximate
> error bounds nothing here, it only describes progress. On a system where the
> method converges slowly the two can differ by an order of magnitude.
>
> The **relative** versions, which is what a stopping test should use, are
> $0.42\%$, $0.49\%$ and $0.74\%$ against the iterate magnitudes.

> [!info] Where the quarter comes from, and when the two agree
> With $\rho = 0.5281$ and the error still contracting geometrically, the error
> $e_4 = \rho\,e_3$ means the last step removed a fraction $1 - \rho = 0.47$ of the
> remaining error. So $|x_4 - x_3| = e_3 - e_4 = (1-\rho)e_3 = e_3(1-\rho)$ while
> $e_4 = \rho e_3$, and the ratio is $\frac{e_4}{e_3 - e_4} = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho} = 1.12$,
> that is $e_4 \approx 1.12\lvert\Delta\rvert$ rather than the quarter observed.
> The gap is because the first three sweeps are still in the transient, before the
> geometric regime settles, and the observed sequence of $x$-errors
> $0.4084, 0.1063, 0.0068, 0.0022$ has ratios $0.26$, $0.06$, $0.33$ that bracket
> $0.53$ only loosely.
>
> So the two error measures agree closely only when the iteration is well into
> its asymptotic regime, and this four-sweep table is not quite there. That is
> worth knowing rather than glossing: the "quarter" is an empirical observation
> from this table, not a theorem, and on a slowly converging system the
> approximate error can overstate the true one by a large factor.

### **[CT-02B Q.2(a)] Using RK2 method with step size $h = 0.2$, calculate $y(0.2)$ and $y(0.4)$. Show both the predictor and corrector calculations at each step.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 - y, \qquad y(0) = 1, \qquad f(x,y) = x^2 - y$$

**The two-stage form.** RK2 in Heun's arrangement is a predictor slope and a
corrector slope averaged over the step:

$$k_1 = f(x_n, y_n), \qquad k_2 = f\left(x_n + h,\ y_n + hk_1\right)$$

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{2}\left(k_1 + k_2\right)$$

**Step 1: $x_0 = 0$ to $x_1 = 0.2$, with $y_0 = 1$.**

Predictor slope:

$$k_1 = 0^2 - 1 = -1$$

Predicted value at the end of the step:

$$y_1^{*} = y_0 + hk_1 = 1 + 0.2(-1) = 0.8$$

Corrector slope, evaluated at the predicted end point:

$$k_2 = (0 + 0.2)^2 - \left(1 + 0.2(-1)\right) = 0.04 - 0.8 = -0.76$$

Update:

$$y_1 = 1 + \frac{0.2}{2}\left(-1 + (-0.76)\right) = 1 + 0.1(-1.76) = 1 - 0.176$$

$$\boxed{y(0.2) \approx 0.8240}$$

**Step 2: $x_1 = 0.2$ to $x_2 = 0.4$, with $y_1 = 0.824$.**

$$k_1 = (0.2)^2 - 0.824 = 0.04 - 0.824 = -0.784$$

$$y_2^{*} = 0.824 + 0.2(-0.784) = 0.824 - 0.1568 = 0.6672$$

$$k_2 = (0.2 + 0.2)^2 - 0.6672 = 0.16 - 0.6672 = -0.5072$$

$$y_2 = 0.824 + \frac{0.2}{2}\left(-0.784 + (-0.5072)\right) = 0.824 + 0.1(-1.2912) = 0.824 - 0.12912$$

$$\boxed{y(0.4) \approx 0.6949}$$

**The results.**

| $x$ | $y$ |
| :--: | --: |
| 0.0 | 1.0000 |
| 0.2 | 0.8240 |
| 0.4 | 0.6949 |

> [!info] Check against the exact solution
> $y' + y = x^2$ has the particular solution $x^2 - 2x + 2$ and homogeneous part
> $Ce^{-x}$. With $y(0) = 1$ we get $C = -1$, so
> $$y = x^2 - 2x + 2 - e^{-x}$$
> and $y(0.2) = 0.04 - 0.4 + 2 - e^{-0.2} = 1.64 - 0.818731 = 0.821269$ and
> $y(0.4) = 0.16 - 0.8 + 2 - e^{-0.4} = 1.36 - 0.670320 = 0.689680$.
>
> So RK2 with $h = 0.2$ overshoots by $0.002731$ at $x = 0.2$ and by $0.005200$ at
> $x = 0.4$. **The error grows as the interval is traversed**, which is the
> signature of a truncated local expansion accumulated step by step, and it is
> worth separating from the effect of the step size.
>
> The step-size effect is tested by halving $h$, not by going further along $x$.
> With $h = 0.1$ the same method gives $0.821928$ and $0.690931$, whose errors are
> $0.000658$ and $0.001251$. The ratios are
> $$0.002731/0.000658 = 4.15, \qquad 0.005200/0.001251 = 4.16$$
> and $4 \approx 2^2$ is the second-order convergence signature. Both ratios land
> on $4.15$ rather than exactly $4$ because a higher-order term still survives at
> the coarser step; the third-order contribution is about four times the
> second-order one at $h = 0.2$.
>
> The comparison worth stating is with RK4 at the same step: **RK2 at $h = 0.2$ is
> off by $0.33\%$ and RK4 at the same step is off by $0.0005\%$**, for two
> function evaluations per step against four.

> [!warning] Three traps in a two-stage method
> **The corrector must be evaluated at the predicted point**, not at the start of
> the step. Writing $k_2 = f(x_n + h, y_n)$ instead of $f(x_n+h, y_n + hk_1)$
> reduces RK2 to the trapezoidal rule applied without the prediction, and it is
> no longer second order; the error then fails to fall by $4$ under halving, which
> is the test that exposes it.
>
> **The predictor must not be used as the answer.** Heun's method reports
> $y_n + \frac{h}{2}(k_1 + k_2)$, not the predicted value $y_n + hk_1$. The two
> differ by $\frac{h}{2}(k_2 - k_1)$; at step 1 that is $0.1(0.24) = 0.024$, which
> is nine times the error of the finished answer. A method that returns its own
> predictor has silently degenerated to Euler.
>
> **RK2 is not one method.** Heun's method averages the two slopes; the midpoint
> method uses the slope at the step midpoint, $k_2 = f\left(x_n + \frac{h}{2},
> y_n + \frac{h}{2}k_1\right)$, with $y_{n+1} = y_n + hk_2$; Ralston's uses $2/3$
> and $1/3$. All are second order but their constants differ, and at $h = 0.2$ on
> this problem they give three different answers:
>
> | arrangement | $y(0.2)$ | $y(0.4)$ | error at $0.2$ |
> | :-- | --: | --: | --: |
> | Heun | 0.824000 | 0.694880 | $+0.002731$ |
> | midpoint | 0.822000 | 0.691240 | $+0.000731$ |
> | Ralston | 0.820148 | 0.687469 | $-0.001121$ |
>
> So the arrangement must be stated whenever an "RK2" answer is quoted. Heun is
> the convention used elsewhere in this course, and the one written above.

### **[CT-02B Q.2(b)] Using the RK4 method calculate $y(0.2)$, also compare result with RK2 method.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x^2 - y, \qquad y(0) = 1, \qquad h = 0.2$$

**The four-stage formula.**

$$k_1 = f(x_n, y_n), \qquad k_2 = f\left(x_n + \frac{h}{2},\ y_n + \frac{h}{2}k_1\right)$$

$$k_3 = f\left(x_n + \frac{h}{2},\ y_n + \frac{h}{2}k_2\right), \qquad k_4 = f(x_n + h,\ y_n + hk_3)$$

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac{h}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right)$$

**Step 1: $x_0 = 0$, $y_0 = 1$, $h = 0.2$.**

$$k_1 = 0^2 - 1 = -1$$

$$k_2 = (0.1)^2 - \left(1 + 0.1(-1)\right) = 0.01 - 0.9 = -0.89$$

$$k_3 = (0.1)^2 - \left(1 + 0.1(-0.89)\right) = 0.01 - 0.911 = -0.901$$

$$k_4 = (0.2)^2 - \left(1 + 0.2(-0.901)\right) = 0.04 - 0.8198 = -0.7798$$

$$y_1 = 1 + \frac{0.2}{6}\left(-1 + 2(-0.89) + 2(-0.901) + (-0.7798)\right)$$

$$= 1 + \frac{0.2}{6}\left(-1 - 1.78 - 1.802 - 0.7798\right) = 1 + \frac{0.2}{6}(-5.3618)$$

$$= 1 - 0.178727$$

$$\boxed{y(0.2) \approx 0.8213 \quad\text{(RK4)}}$$

**The comparison.**

| method | $y(0.2)$ | absolute error against $0.821269$ |
| :-- | --: | --: |
| exact | 0.821269 | 0 (reference) |
| RK4, $h = 0.2$ | 0.821273 | $0.000004$ |
| RK2 Heun, $h = 0.2$ | 0.824000 | $0.002731$ |
| Euler, $h = 0.2$ | 0.800000 | $0.021269$ |

$$\boxed{\text{RK4 error} \approx 4\times10^{-6}, \quad \text{RK2 error} \approx 2.73\times10^{-3}}$$

RK4 is about **670 times** more accurate at the same step. One RK4 step costs
the same four function evaluations as two RK2 steps of $h = 0.1$, and that is
the comparison that decides the choice: two RK2 steps of $h = 0.1$ give
$y(0.2) = 0.821928$, whose error $0.000658$ is still **160 times** RK4's, for
the same number of evaluations. RK2's error falls as $h^2$ and RK4's as $h^4$,
so the gap widens as $h$ shrinks and only a change of method closes it.

Euler's $0.800000$ is included because it is the predictor RK2 discards. The
gap between it and the exact solution, $0.021269$, is about eight times RK2's
own error, and that ratio is the share of the work the corrector is doing.

> [!info] The four $k$ values are one row of a table, and the row must be recomputed
> Every $k_i$ is $f(x, y)$ at the $x$ and $y$ printed beside it in the same row,
> not at the start of the step. At step 1, $k_2$ is evaluated at
> $(0.1,\ 0.9)$ and $k_3$ at $(0.1,\ 0.911)$, and those two arguments differ by
> $0.011$ because they come from different stages. A table in which $k_2$ and
> $k_3$ share an argument was computed by a modified Euler step, not by RK4, and
> its result will be wrong in the fourth digit while still looking reasonable.
>
> The independent check is the exact solution again. $y(0.2) = 1.64 - e^{-0.2} =
> 0.821269$, and RK4 with one step of $h = 0.2$ gives $0.821273$: four correct
> decimal places from a single step, which is what a fourth-order method promises
> and what RK2 at the same step cannot approach.

### **[CT-04 Q.1] Let $p$, $q$, $r$ be propositions. Using a complete truth table, establish that $[(p \to q) \land (q \to r)] \to (p \to r)$ is a tautology.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

**The statement.** Write the antecedent and conclusion separately:

$$A = (p \to q) \land (q \to r), \qquad C = p \to r, \qquad E = A \to C$$

Using $P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$, the whole statement reduces to

$$E = \neg\left[(p \to q) \land (q \to r)\right] \lor (p \to r) = \neg(p \to q) \lor \neg(q \to r) \lor (p \to r)$$

which expands to $\neg(\neg p \lor q) \lor \neg(\neg q \lor r) \lor (\neg p \lor r)$ and then to

$$E = (p \land \neg q) \lor (q \land \neg r) \lor (\neg p \lor r)$$

For $E$ to be false, all three disjuncts must be false, which requires
$p \land \neg q$, $q \land \neg r$ and $\neg p \lor r$ simultaneously. The first
needs $q$ false and the second needs $q$ true: a contradiction. So $E$ can never
be false, which is the argument the table below makes explicit.

**The complete truth table.**

| $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to r$ | $A = (p \to q) \land (q \to r)$ | $p \to r$ | $E = A \to C$ |
| :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| T | T | T | T | T | T | T | **T** |
| T | T | F | T | F | F | F | **T** |
| T | F | T | F | T | F | T | **T** |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F | **T** |
| F | T | T | T | T | T | T | **T** |
| F | T | F | T | F | F | T | **T** |
| F | F | T | T | T | T | T | **T** |
| F | F | F | T | T | T | T | **T** |

$$\boxed{E \text{ is true in all 8 rows, so } [(p \to q) \land (q \to r)] \to (p \to r) \text{ is a tautology}}$$

**What the table shows.** The interesting column is $A$. It is false in rows 2, 3,
4 and 6, and in each of those the implication $A \to C$ is **vacuously true**
regardless of $C$. So the eight rows collapse to four that actually need
checking: rows 1, 5, 7 and 8 are the only ones where $A$ is true, and $p \to r$ is
true in all four. The only rows where $E$ could fail are those with $A$ true and
$p \to r$ false, and there are none.

> [!info] This is transitivity, and the argument form is modus ponens
> The statement *is* transitivity of implication: if $p$ implies $q$ and $q$
> implies $r$, then $p$ implies $r$. A tautology is a theorem of propositional
> logic precisely when some argument form proves it, and this one is proved by
> **modus ponens** on $p$: from $p$ and $p \to q$ get $q$; from $q$ and
> $q \to r$ get $r$; hence $p \to r$.
>
> That is a genuine second route, not a restatement: the truth table checks eight
> cases, while modus ponens derives the conclusion from the premises in two steps
> and is independent of how many variables there are. Agreement between them is
> the check worth quoting.

> [!warning] The row that gets marked wrong, and the cell to read twice
> Row 4, $p = \mathrm{T}, q = \mathrm{F}, r = \mathrm{F}$. Here $p \to q$ is F, since
> the antecedent holds and the conclusion does not. But $q \to r$ is **T**: both
> $q$ and $r$ are false, and $\neg q \lor r$ holds because $q$ is false. That is
> the vacuously true implication, the one that is true because its antecedent is
> false.
>
> So $A$ is F, and $E$ is vacuously T. It is tempting to read the false $A$ as
> making $E$ false, or to read the two F's in the middle as agreeing. The rule is
> that $A \to C$ is false only when $A$ is true **and** $C$ is false, so a false
> antecedent can never falsify an implication.

### **[CT-04 Q.2(a)] Translate the requirement into propositional logic.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

> *"A student can start the examination if and only if the student is
> authenticated and the examination server is available."*

With $p$: authenticated, $q$: server available, $r$: can start the examination:

$$\boxed{r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)}$$

The connective matters and is fixed by one word. "**if and only if**" is a
biconditional, $\leftrightarrow$, and the compound $p \land q$ is its whole right
side. So the requirement says both

$$(p \land q) \to r \qquad \text{and} \qquad r \to (p \land q)$$

Reading it as a one-way implication, $(p \land q) \to r$, states only half the
requirement and permits a student to start the examination who is not
authenticated, which is precisely what the sentence forbids.

**The complete truth table.**

| $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $p \land q$ | $r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)$ |
| :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| T | T | T | T | **T** |
| T | T | F | T | **F** |
| T | F | T | F | **F** |
| T | F | F | F | **T** |
| F | T | T | F | **F** |
| F | T | F | F | **T** |
| F | F | T | F | **F** |
| F | F | F | F | **T** |

$$\boxed{r \leftrightarrow (p \land q) \text{ is true in 4 of 8 rows, so it is contingent}}$$

It is not a tautology: rows 2, 3 and 5 are false. Nor is it a contradiction: rows
1, 4, 6 and 8 are true. A statement that permits four assignments is contingent,
and that is the honest classification of this requirement.

> [!info] What the four true rows mean
> The formula holds when $r$ matches $p \land q$: the student may start exactly
> when both conditions hold. Rows 1 and 4 are the interesting pair, because they
> are the only ones where $r$ and $p \land q$ agree, and they are what the
> requirement asserts and nothing else. Rows 2 and 5 are the states the system
> must **prevent**: an authenticated student with an available server who cannot
> start, and an unauthenticated student who can.
>
> The second is the security case, and it is the reason the biconditional is
> written at all. A one-way implication would leave row 5 permitted.

### **[CT-04 Q.2(b)] Rewrite the resulting expression using only $\neg$, $\lor$, and $\land$.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

Start from $r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)$ and replace the biconditional by its
two implications:

$$r \leftrightarrow (p \land q) \equiv \big[(p \land q) \to r\big] \land \big[r \to (p \land q)\big]$$

Replace each implication by $P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q$:

$$\equiv \big[\neg(p \land q) \lor r\big] \land \big[\neg r \lor (p \land q)\big]$$

Apply De Morgan's law $\neg(p \land q) \equiv \neg p \lor \neg q$ to the first
bracket:

$$\boxed{\big[(\neg p \lor \neg q) \lor r\big] \land \big[\neg r \lor (p \land q)\big]}$$

$$\boxed{\equiv (\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r) \land (\neg r \lor p \land q)}$$

which uses only $\neg$, $\lor$ and $\land$, as required.

**Step by step, with the law named at each move:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)
  &\equiv \big[(p \land q) \to r\big] \land \big[r \to (p \land q)\big]
    && \text{biconditional as two implications}\\
  &\equiv \big[\neg(p \land q) \lor r\big] \land \big[\neg r \lor (p \land q)\big]
    && P \to Q \equiv \neg P \lor Q\\
  &\equiv \big[(\neg p \lor \neg q) \lor r\big] \land \big[\neg r \lor (p \land q)\big]
    && \text{De Morgan}\\
  &\equiv (\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r) \land (\neg r \lor p \land q)
    && \text{associativity of } \lor
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!info] Check by truth table, on all eight rows
> The rewritten formula and $r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)$ are true in exactly
> the same four rows, 1, 4, 6 and 8, and false in the other four. Two formulas
> with the same table are the same formula, so the rewrite is correct.
>
> A second check is available in the other direction. The final form reads as a
> requirement on the system rather than as algebra: $\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r$
> says *something must fail or the student may start*, and
> $\neg r \lor p \land q$ says *if the student starts, both must hold*. Those are
> the two halves of "if and only if", recovered from the rewritten form.

> [!warning] The bracket that must not be dropped
> $(\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r) \land (\neg r \lor p \land q)$ is **not** the same
> as $\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r \land \neg r \lor p \land q$ without the outer
> brackets, because $\lor$ binds more weakly than $\land$. The outer conjunction
> is the whole statement: drop it and the formula becomes trivially true in rows
> where only one half is satisfied, and the requirement silently weakens to a
> single implication.
>
> Note also that $\neg r \lor p \land q$ parses as
> $\neg r \lor (p \land q)$, which is what is intended, since $\land$ binds
> tighter than $\lor$. Both readings are correct as written; they are not the
> same formula, and the one to quote is the bracketed pair.

### **[CT-04 Q.2(c)] Construct a truth table for the resulting expression.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

The resulting expression is
$F = (\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r) \land (\neg r \lor p \land q)$. Build it one
column at a time, which is the only way to be sure each row is right.

| $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | $\neg p$ | $\neg q$ | $\neg r$ | $p \land q$ | $\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r$ | $\neg r \lor p \land q$ | $F$ |
| :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| T | T | T | F | F | F | T | **T** | **T** | **T** |
| T | T | F | F | F | T | T | **F** | **T** | **F** |
| T | F | T | F | T | F | F | **T** | **F** | **F** |
| T | F | F | F | T | T | F | **T** | **T** | **T** |
| F | T | T | T | F | F | F | **T** | **F** | **F** |
| F | T | F | T | F | T | F | **T** | **T** | **T** |
| F | F | T | T | T | F | F | **T** | **F** | **F** |
| F | F | F | T | T | T | F | **T** | **T** | **T** |

$$\boxed{F \text{ is true in rows 1, 4, 6, 8 and false in rows 2, 3, 5, 7}}$$

$$\boxed{F \text{ is contingent: it is not a tautology and not a contradiction}}$$

> [!info] Reading the table back into the requirement
> The four true rows are the four states the specification permits. They are
> exactly the rows where $r$ agrees with $p \land q$, which is what
> $r \leftrightarrow (p \land q)$ asserts, so the rewrite has preserved the
> meaning and not merely the form.
>
> The false rows name the three defects a test plan should look for:
>
> | row | $p$ | $q$ | $r$ | what it is |
> | :--: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-- |
> | 2 | T | T | F | authenticated, server up, cannot start |
> | 3 | T | F | T | **can start with the server down** |
> | 5 | F | T | T | **can start without authenticating** |
>
> Rows 3 and 5 are the security failures, and they are exactly what a one-way
> implication would have allowed.

> [!warning] The column that catches the commonest slip
> The middle column $\neg p \lor \neg q \lor r$ is T in seven of eight rows and
> false only in row 2, where $p = q = \mathrm{T}$ and $r = \mathrm{F}$: both
> conditions hold and the student still cannot start. Row 2 is the only state the
> first bracket alone forbids.
>
> The second bracket $\neg r \lor p \land q$ is false in rows 3 and 5, both of
> which have $r = \mathrm{T}$ with at least one condition unmet. So each bracket
> rules out a different failure, and **the statement is the conjunction of the two
> because the requirement has two halves**. Reading it as a disjunction, which is
> the sign error, admits either half being wrong.

### **[CT-04 Q.3(a)] Translate all three statements into propositional logic.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

With $p$: the sensor is active, $q$: the controller receives data.

**Statement 1.** "If the sensor is active, then the controller receives data."

$$S_1 = p \to q$$

**Statement 2.** "The controller does not receive data **unless** the sensor is
active."

The word "unless" is the whole difficulty. "$B$ unless $A$" means *if not $A$,
then $B$*, which is $\neg A \to B$, and equivalently $A \lor B$. Here
$A$ = the sensor is active, $p$; $B$ = the controller receives data, $q$. So

$$S_2 = \neg p \to q \quad\equiv\quad p \lor q \quad\equiv\quad q \to p$$

**Statement 3.** "The sensor is active if and only if the controller receives
data."

$$S_3 = p \leftrightarrow q$$

> [!warning] "Unless" is not "if and only if", and the difference is the whole question
> Read "does not receive data unless the sensor is active" as
> "$q \to p$": receiving data requires an active sensor. Read instead as
> "$p \to q$" it collapses into statement 1 and the second statement adds nothing,
> which is a sign that the reading is wrong.
>
> The three statements are therefore $p \to q$, $q \to p$ and $p \leftrightarrow q$,
> and the first two are already the two halves of the third. Statements 1 and 2
> are **redundant**. That is not a defect in the question, it is the point: the
> specification states its central requirement twice, and a student who notices
> that has understood what the third statement adds, which is nothing.
>
> The related trap is reading "unless" as "$p \leftrightarrow q$". That would
> make the three statements a single biconditional and the specification
> vacuous.

### **[CT-04 Q.3(b)] Form a single compound proposition by taking the conjunction of the three requirements.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

$$C = S_1 \land S_2 \land S_3 = (p \to q) \land (q \to p) \land (p \leftrightarrow q)$$

Replace the biconditional by its two implications:

$$C = (p \to q) \land (q \to p) \land (p \to q) \land (q \to p)$$

and since the first pair already contains both directions,

$$\boxed{C = (p \to q) \land (q \to p)}$$

$$\boxed{C = (\neg p \lor q) \land (\neg q \lor p)}$$

$$\boxed{C = (\neg p \lor q) \land (\neg q \lor p) \equiv (p \land q) \lor (\neg p \land \neg q)}$$

The last line is the distributive form: the conjunction is false in every row
where exactly one of $p, q$ is true, and true only where they agree.

> [!info] Check by simplification, and by noting what was thrown away
> Simplifying $C$ gives $(p \land q) \lor (\neg p \land \neg q)$, which is exactly
> $p \leftrightarrow q$. So the conjunction of all three requirements is
> **equivalent to the third one alone**, because the third subsumes the first two.
>
> That is the correct and expected result, and it is worth stating plainly rather
> than presenting six conjuncts and leaving the reader to find the redundancy.
> The specification is satisfiable, and how it is satisfiable is the content of
> part (d).

### **[CT-04 Q.3(c)] Construct the complete truth table from (b).**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

| $p$ | $q$ | $p \to q$ | $q \to p$ | $S_3 = p \leftrightarrow q$ | $C = S_1 \land S_2 \land S_3$ |
| :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| T | T | T | T | T | **T** |
| T | F | F | T | F | **F** |
| F | T | T | F | F | **F** |
| F | F | T | T | T | **T** |

$$\boxed{C \text{ is true in rows 1 and 4, so it is contingent}}$$

$$\boxed{C \text{ is equivalent to } p \leftrightarrow q}$$

> [!info] The two true rows are the two consistent states
> Row 1: the sensor is active **and** the controller receives data. The system is
> working as intended.
>
> Row 4: the sensor is inactive **and** the controller receives nothing. The
> sensor is off, so nothing should arrive, and nothing does.
>
> Rows 2 and 3 are the failure modes: an active sensor with no data, which is a
> dead or disconnected sensor, and an inactive sensor with data, which means data
> is arriving from a sensor the system believes is off. The second of those is the
> dangerous one, since the controller would be acting on data it should treat as
> impossible.
>
> The table is also the check on part (b): the conjunction column matches the
> biconditional column in all four rows, which is what "the third statement
> subsumes the first two" means in a table.

### **[CT-04 Q.3(d)] Establish the truth assignments that satisfy all three requirements simultaneously.**

**[Marks: not printed in source]**

Reading the $C$ column of the table in part (c), the conjunction is true in rows 1
and 4 only:

| assignment | $p$ | $q$ | meaning | satisfies all three |
| :-- | :-: | :-: | :-- | :-: |
| 1 | **T** | **T** | sensor active, controller receives data | **yes** |
| 4 | **F** | **F** | sensor inactive, controller receives nothing | **yes** |

$$\boxed{\text{The specification is satisfiable, and the satisfying assignments are exactly } (p,q) = (\mathrm{T},\mathrm{T}) \text{ and } (\mathrm{F},\mathrm{F})}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Equivalently: the requirements hold if and only if } p = q}$$

**Check that no other assignment works.** Row 2, $p = \mathrm{T}, q = \mathrm{F}$,
fails statement 2, since data does arrive while the sensor is inactive. Row 3,
$p = \mathrm{F}, q = \mathrm{T}$, fails statement 1, since data arrives with no
active sensor. Those two are the only rows excluded, and they are excluded by
different statements, which is what makes the exclusion complete.

> [!info] Two ways to confirm this without the table
> **By cases on $p$.** If $p$ is true, statement 1 forces $q$ true. If $p$ is
> false, statement 2 reads $\neg p \to q$, whose antecedent is true, so it forces
> $q$ false. Either way $q = p$, giving exactly the two assignments above.
>
> **By the simplified form.** Part (b) gave $C \equiv (p \land q) \lor (\neg p
> \land \neg q)$, and $C$ holds precisely when $p$ and $q$ agree. Agreement of two
> propositions is exactly two assignments.
>
> All three routes agree, and the answer is a pair of assignments rather than a
> single state, which is the substantive finding: **the specification permits the
> system to be idle.** Nothing in the three requirements forces the sensor to be
> active.

> [!warning] The engineering conclusion, and what the specification fails to say
> If a system is required to be *monitoring* at all times, then $p$ must be true,
> and only row 1 survives: $(p, q) = (\mathrm{T}, \mathrm{T})$. But none of the
> three statements says that. They constrain the relationship between sensor and
> controller, not the state of the sensor.
>
> This is the substantive defect in the requirement, and it is the kind of thing a
> specification review exists to find. A fourth statement, "the sensor is active",
> or the operational condition that the system is powered and enabled, would close
> it. An idle state that satisfies every written requirement is not a contradiction
> in the logic; it is a gap in the requirement, and the truth table is what makes
> it visible.
